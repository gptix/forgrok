* Legal frame captured in-session
** Taking the base photograph
- Standing on a public street or sidewalk, photographing what a passerby can see, is generally lawful in the US.
- "No photographs" signs on private lawns do not create a general camera ban for people who stay in the street.
- Do not enter the driveway, yard, or shoot a backyard that is not visible from the public way. No drone-over-lot assumption.

** Copyright in the building
- Architectural works after 1 Dec 1990 are copyrightable.
- 17 USC 120(a): once the building is constructed and located in or ordinarily visible from a public place, that copyright does *not* stop making, distributing, or publicly displaying pictures of it.
- The photographer owns copyright in *their* photograph.
- Separate copyright can still exist in art *on* the house (mural, sculpture, distinctive stained glass treated as artwork).

** Georgia image / publicity law (people, not porches)
- Georgia recognizes common-law appropriation / right of publicity: name or likeness used without consent for financial gain (e.g. *Pavesich*, *Bullard*, *MLK Center*).
- A typical house facade is not a person's likeness.
- Faces, children, or a recognizable person in a window *are* likeness problems if used to sell.

** The remaining marketing risk (the live issue)
The caption can deny "we worked here." Viewers can still read a product-on-this-porch photo as *placement*.

Risk rises when all of these are true:

- Ad runs locally (Cobb / Atlanta), where neighbors know the stoop.
- Porch is distinctive (columns, swing, tile, paint, wreath).
- Pumpkins look *installed on that real porch*, not illustrated.
- The thing being sold is porch décor or installation.

Risk falls when:

- Architecture is generic or heavily stylized / illustrated.
- House is not geographically pinned.
- Disclaimer states composite / not a customer property.
- Stage is a porch the project *controls* (own house, rented facade, 3D).

A disclaimer helps more than silence. It does not make a lookalike local placement bulletproof.

Theories that can still be argued even with the user's constraints:

- Implied endorsement / unfair competition / Lanham Act false association if a viewer thinks the household sponsors the product.
- Nuisance-value homeowner complaint.
- Separate copyright in composited pumpkin *assets* if those assets were scraped from another vendor.

GREC advertising rules apply if a *licensed* agent is advertising a specific property for sale. That is a different fact pattern from pumpkin décor ads.

** Practical rule recorded in-session
Safer: public-street exterior, no people, no work claim, no readable address.

Get a written property release if the house is the hero of the ad, the street is named, the copy implies a project or client, or anyone recognizable appears.

Safest creative path recorded: build or rent a porch set, or use 3D, and market *that*.

* Production pipeline (proposed, not built)
** 1. Capture
- Public sidewalk or street only.
- Prefer overcast or open shade so composites do not scream "noon Georgia sun + stock pumpkin."
- Shoot extra plates: empty stoop, ground plane, step edges, for later shadows.
- Do not publish raws that still show numbers or faces.

** 2. Strip identifiers
- Address digits, script house names, mailbox flags with names, plates, school stickers, faces, distinctive yard art that IDs the lot.
- Crop tighter than the lot lines if the house form itself is a fingerprint.

** 3. Composite pumpkins
- Use only assets the project owns or has licensed (own photos, purchased stock, original 3D).
- Do not lift product shots from another seller's catalog.
- Match perspective, scale on the tread, and a single light direction.
- If the result still "reads as a photo of a real install," treat it operationally as an install photo for risk, even if built in software.

** 4. Caption / collateral rules
Mandatory negatives (user):

- No "we did this porch."
- No "our client on [street]."
- No "references from this home."

Recommended positives (session advice, not user-mandated):

- "Composite image. Not an actual customer property."
- No map pin, no "Vinings homes we dress," no before/after of a known lot.

** 5. Review before print
If the porch is recognizable *and* the product is porch displays, get a lawyer to look at the actual image + copy before a large print run. Session advice, not a retainer.
