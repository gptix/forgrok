* Summary

Brian Roemmele traces the history and practical value of brainwave entrainment via flashing lights (photic driving). He begins with the 1934 discovery by Edgar Adrian and Bryan Matthews,
follows the extensive Soviet research programs on photic driving and electrosleep, examines the 1990s commercial realization in devices such as the Zygon SuperMind
(and the key Tye Rubins patents), recounts his own multi-decade personal use for accelerated learning, and connects the lineage to modern MIT clinical work on 40 Hz
light stimulation for Alzheimer’s disease. The essay argues that a technique long dismissed as “New Age” gadgetry rests on solid, century-old neurophysiological foundations
and is now re-entering legitimate clinical and performance contexts.

* Key Claims & Concepts

- Photic driving (frequency-following response to rhythmic light) was first documented in 1934 by Adrian & Matthews using early EEG.
- Soviet researchers developed the phenomenon extensively for diagnostic, therapeutic, and performance applications (anxiety, sleep, nervous-system typology, electrosleep).
- The 1990s Zygon SuperMind and related “Learning Machine” devices were direct commercial implementations of this science, protected by a family of patents filed by Tye Rubins (US5306228A and continuations).
- Roemmele used these systems for years as a practical tool to reach theta/alpha states that improved concentration, reduced mental chatter, and accelerated technical learning.
- Modern MIT research on 40 Hz sensory stimulation for Alzheimer’s represents a rigorous clinical return to the same underlying mechanism.
- The technique is neither mystical nor obsolete; it is a controllable method of shifting brain state with measurable historical and contemporary support.
