What started as scattered, carefully guarded inquiries in several
countries gradually converged. Different traditions, different
experimental approaches, and different theoretical languages all pointed
toward the same elusive domain, an ability of living systems to interact
at a distance through means no conventional instrument could fully
account for. The implications were too significant, and the results too
unstable, to be left in the open.

By the middle decades of the century the most concentrated and
best-resourced effort had taken shape in one particular state. There,
the scattered threads were drawn together under sustained institutional
support. Specialized instruments were built, gifted subjects were
studied under controlled conditions, and theoretical models were refined
in secret. The scale of the investment, the breadth of the inquiry, and
the seriousness with which the work was pursued set it apart from
everything that had come before.

Even now, decades later, large portions of that culminating program
remain sealed or only partially recovered. The technology—if it can be
called that—was never fully revealed, never fully explained, and never
completely dismissed. What survives are fragments, translated summaries,
and the persistent sense that something real was being tracked in those
closed rooms, something that still resists easy understanding.

For decades, separate but strikingly parallel lines of rigorous
scientific inquiry have been mapping the capacity of focused human
consciousness to influence biological systems, physical matter, and even
distant physiology. These efforts span continents and methodologies:
large-scale collective intention tests coordinated across thousands of
participants, meticulous Chinese laboratory measurements of external qi
emitted by trained practitioners, and sensitive Japanese biophysical
recordings of ultra-weak photon emissions and remote physiological
correlations during mental concentration and qigong practice. Together
they form a coherent body of evidence that intention is not merely
subjective experience but a directed force capable of producing
measurable, reproducible effects.

This work builds on ancient traditions while employing modern
instrumentation, statistical rigor, and controlled protocols. It
challenges entrenched assumptions that consciousness is a passive
byproduct of brain chemistry. The skeptics who declare such phenomena
impossible rarely engage the actual experiments or data. They offer
pronouncements rather than replications. Meanwhile, researchers continue
to accumulate findings across plants, water, cells, animals, and human
physiology that demand serious attention. The trajectory points toward
profound changes in how medicine understands healing, prevention, and
the role of mind and energy in human health.

Historical Roots and the Scientific Turn

The Chinese tradition of qigong dates back thousands of years.
Practitioners cultivate and direct qi, the vital life energy, through
movement, breath, and yi, or focused intention. External qi emission,
known as wai qi, involves projecting this energy outward, often for
healing purposes at a distance and without physical contact. Traditional
texts and clinical lore describe effects on pain, immunity, and
vitality.

In the 1980s, China launched a major scientific effort to study these
phenomena objectively. Laboratories measured electromagnetic fields,
infrared emissions, heat, and other signals during external qi sessions.
Researchers documented effects on seed germination and plant growth,
alterations in water molecular structure detectable by infrared
spectroscopy, and influences on cellular and animal models. Reviews such
as the 2004 analytic survey by K.W. Chen catalogued hundreds of studies
across physical, chemical, and biological detectors, noting consistent
correlates that resisted simple placebo explanations in many cases.
Masters such as Yan Xin conducted external qi experiments showing
influences on molecular structures and signaling pathways relevant to
cancer cells.

Parallel developments occurred in Japan. Mikio Yamamoto, a physicist who
developed Japan’s first positron emission tomography scanner and

temperature, biophotons, infrared, respiration, and hemoglobin changes
during qigong, mental concentration, and remote influence tasks.
Yamamoto also served as president of the International Society of Life
Information Science, fostering international forums on human potential
phenomena.

Soviet-Era Research on Directed Consciousness, Bioenergy, and Intention-Like Effects: A Parallel Line of Evidence

The Soviet Union maintained one of the most extensive state-supported
programs investigating phenomena now recognized as central to the
convergence of intention research, vital energy systems, and measurable
influences on biological and physical matter. Rebranded as
“psychotronics,” “bioenergetics,” “biocommunications,” or
“energoinformational exchange” to align with official ideology, this
work spanned telepathy (long-distance biological signal transmission),
psychokinesis (PK or anomalous perturbation), biofields, human
emissions, and distant effects on plants, water, and living systems. It
ran in cycles of heavy funding and occasional suppression from the 1920s
through the 1980s, with estimates of total investment reaching hundreds
of millions to as much as one billion dollars when including classified
military and intelligence efforts.

This body of research forms another major parallel to the Chinese
external qi studies on energy emission affecting distant targets,
Yamamoto’s Japanese bio-emission and remote physiological measurements,
and the global collective intention experiments that demonstrate focused
mind influencing subtle properties of matter and biology. Soviet
scientists documented non-local signals, human-generated fields or
emissions modulating other systems, and intention-like mental influence
producing statistically significant changes in the same categories of
targets: plant growth and biochemistry, water chemistry, and
physiological or healing outcomes. Much of the work occurred in academic
institutes under Academy of Sciences oversight, with military interest
in potential applications.

Historical Context and State Involvement

Early foundations trace to the 1920s with committees for psychical
research and programs at institutions like Bechterev’s Brain Institute
in Leningrad and the Biophysics Laboratory of the Academy of Sciences in
Moscow. A 1932–1937 Defense Commissar initiative explored biological
signal transmission and remote influence. After a period of repression,
a 1963 Kremlin edict prioritized biological sciences, including
rebranded parapsychology research. Funding and coordination intensified
in the 1970s–1980s through the State Committee for Science and
Technology, with open programs alongside classified efforts by the
Ministry of Defense and KGB. Researchers operated under materialist
framing, seeking physical carriers such as electromagnetic or
“high-penetrating” emissions rather than supernatural explanations.

Declassified U.S. intelligence assessments, including the 1975 Defense
Intelligence Agency report on Soviet and Czechoslovakian parapsychology
research, document the scale and multidisciplinary nature of the work.
Post-Soviet analyses, such as the overview of unconventional research in
the USSR and Russia, confirm consistent patterns across decades despite
political shifts.

Key Research Areas and Specific Studies

1. Telepathy and Long-Distance Biological Signal Transmission
Soviet programs tested non-local communication between humans, animals,
and plants. Experiments at Bechterev’s Brain Institute and the Moscow
Biophysics Laboratory in the 1930s involved transferring visual images
and remote suggestion. These were replicated in the 1960s, including
1965–1967 rabbit studies over 7 km where implanted electrodes recorded
behavioral and physiological responses with high statistical
significance (over 50% matches in some trials, far above chance).
Signals persisted through Faraday cages, pointing to non-electromagnetic
“high-penetrating” radiation. Researchers such as L.L. Vasiliev and V.P.
Perov contributed to this line. Later work extended to dermo-optical
perception and bioinformation exchange.

These studies parallel remote influence components in both Chinese
external qi healing and Yamamoto-group remote action experiments
measuring physiological changes (EDA, skin temperature) in shielded
receivers during focused sending.

2. Psychokinesis (PK), Anomalous Perturbation (AP), and Bio-PK on
Biological Systems
A major focus involved mental or operator influence on physical and
living targets, often called bio-AP or distant mental effect. Targets
included microorganisms, plants (morphology, biochemistry, growth),
animals, and humans (physiology and behavior). Many studies reported
statistically significant results.

At Moscow State University’s Division of Biophysics, researchers
including Andrei B. Rubin, Tatyana Ye. Krendeleva, Sergei I. Pogosyan,
and Valery B. Turovetsky conducted pilot studies on distant mental
influence. In one series, a sensitive/healer (B. Verbitsky) attempted to
alter the pH/acidity of water or solutions from 20–30 cm distance for
3–5 minutes. The target was in a thermostat-controlled water bath and
continuously stirred magnetically. Measurable pH changes occurred across
temperatures from 5°C to 48°C, but only under active stirring
conditions. The operator described it as a learned skill, initially
producing larger shifts and later more controlled smaller ones. Related
work examined relaxation characteristics of water protons in sprouting
seeds under extrasensory influence.165

Other programs studied distant effects on plant growth and bioelectrical
activity (e.g., cucumber plants showing significant responses to mental
stimulation versus inhibition, p < 0.01 in some trials by Yuri S.
Dolin’s group). These directly echo Chinese external qi effects on seed
germination and plant growth, as well as McTaggart intention experiment
targets of accelerated seed growth and water chemistry shifts.

3. Biofields, Bioplasma, and Human Emissions
Victor Inyushin at Kazakh State University (Alma-Ata) developed the
bioplasma hypothesis: living systems contain an organized “bioplasma” of
ions, free protons, and electrons forming a fifth state of matter,
constantly renewed by cellular chemical processes and in constant
motion. This field was proposed as underlying auras, health states, and
potentially non-local interactions. Research linked it to diagnostic and
therapeutic applications, with parallels to energy emission concepts.135

Complementary work by S.Y. Turlygin and others in the 1930s–1940s
detected microwave-like radiation (wavelength ~1.8–2.1 mm) emitted by
humans. These emissions were not deflected by electric fields or blocked
by lead in expected ways and were shown to influence nervous system
excitation, emotional states, and organ functions in recipient systems.
Later programs examined non-ionizing emissions from biological objects
and their physiological impacts.

This biophysical framing of human-generated fields or emissions aligns
closely with Yamamoto’s measurements of biophoton changes during mental
concentration and qigong, as well as frequency emissions (2–30 Hz)
recorded during external qi or healing intention.

4. Kirlian Photography and Visualization of Energy Fields
Semyon Kirlian (with Valentina Kirlian) developed high-frequency,
high-voltage photography in the Soviet Union starting in the
1930s–1940s. It captured corona discharges or “auras” around living
organisms, revealing patterns linked to health, emotional states, and
acupuncture points (often brighter). Soviet researchers explored its use
for diagnostics and studied changes under various conditions, including
healing or intentional states. Phantom leaf effects (continued glow or
pattern after part of the leaf was removed) were investigated, though
interpretations varied. The technique provided visual evidence of
dynamic fields around biological systems and was integrated into broader
bioenergetics research.131

5. Generators and Instrumental Psychotronics
Researchers developed devices to accumulate or generate “bioenergy” or
related fields. Robert Pavlita’s psychotronic generators (based on shape
and form) reportedly accelerated plant germination and purified
contaminated water. Later torsion field or spin-torsion programs
(associated with A.E. Akimov and others) explored influences on
crystallization, seed states, and material properties. These extended
the study of directed or structured energy effects beyond direct human
operators.

Connections Across Research Traditions and Implications

Soviet psychotronics research supplies independent, multi-decade
evidence from a major scientific power that directed mental processes
and human bioenergy can produce measurable effects on plants, water
chemistry, and distant physiological systems. The emphasis on physical
carriers (emissions, fields, high-penetrating radiation) and rigorous
instrumentation mirrors the detector-based approaches in Chinese
external qi studies and Yamamoto’s multi-parameter protocols
(biophotons, EDA, temperature, optical topography). The targets overlap
precisely with those in McTaggart’s global intention experiments:
accelerated or altered plant/seed responses, water property shifts, and
healing or physiological modulation through focused intention.

State support enabled scale and resources unavailable in many Western
efforts of the era, while the rebranding and materialist framing allowed
open publication in certain journals alongside classified work.
Post-Soviet continuation in torsion and related fields shows persistence
of the inquiry.

Skeptics often dismiss the entire domain as pseudoscience, internal
scams to loot funds or Cold War exaggeration without engaging the
specific protocols, statistical outcomes, or declassified documentation.
Actual Soviet research occurred in recognized institutes, produced
peer-reviewed or deposited reports, and included controls such as
distance, shielding, thermostats, and stirring. Replication challenges
exist across all subtle energy fields, yet the pattern of positive
findings in bio-PK on biological targets and emission studies provides
convergent support when viewed alongside Chinese and Japanese lines.

The Historical Arc: State-Scale Investigation of the Subtle

The research is divided into three major cycles of state-supported work
(1917–1937, 1955–1980, 1980–2003), each interrupted by political rupture
yet each advancing the instrumentalization of the phenomenon. In the
first cycle, researchers at Bechterev’s Brain Institute and Lazarev’s
biophysics laboratory already distinguished the emission from ordinary
electromagnetism.

Faraday-cage experiments showed that telepathic and biological-signal
effects continued unabated, forcing the conclusion that the carrier was
“high-penetrating.” S.Y. Turlygin’s meticulous optical-style experiments
(lead screens, mirrors, diffraction gratings, electric-field capacitors)
yielded a coherent picture: the radiation reflected, diffracted, and
produced measurable delays through dense material, yet remained
undeviated by electrostatic fields. Wavelength estimates fell in the
1.8–2.1 mm range—long enough to interact with biological structures,
short enough to be non-ionizing.

The Physical Character of the Emission

The consistent experimental signature is striking:

- It penetrates Faraday cages, lead, and ordinary electromagnetic
  shielding.
- It exhibits optical-like behavior (reflection, diffraction, measurable

- It is not deflected by static electric fields.
- It interacts with spin systems and material microstructure

- It carries information capable of influencing biological processes at

In the later theoretical framing these properties were gathered under

For a reader of classical Qi literature the resonance is immediate. Qi

Biological and Informational Depth

The documented effects form a coherent pattern:

- Accelerated or altered germination of seeds.
- Clarification and structuring of water.
- Measurable physiological responses in microorganisms, plants, animals,

- Pre-solar-activity bacterial metachromasia (Chizhevsky–Velchover
  effect).
- Long-distance biological signal transfer that survives electromagnetic

The 1975 Defense Intelligence Agency Assessment: “Soviet and Czechoslovakian Parapsychology Research”

Document: DST-1810S-387-75 This is one of the most thorough Western
intelligence assessments of Soviet-bloc research into what the West
called parapsychology and what the Soviets and Czechs preferred to term
biocommunications, psychotronics, psychoenergetics, or biophysical
effects. The report draws on open scientific literature, intelligence
reporting, books, journals, newspapers, and personal communications. It
systematically reviews decades of work, theoretical frameworks,
experimental results, instrumentation, key personnel, facilities, and
potential applications.

The Soviet and Czechoslovakian programs represented a sustained,
multi-decade, state-supported scientific enterprise. Significant
resources—estimates in the report include figures on the order of 20
million rubles in the early 1970s for bioinformation research alone,
alongside institutional laboratories, specialized instrumentation
development, and the involvement of physiologists, physicists,
cyberneticists, and highly selected experimental subjects—were devoted
to quantifying phenomena that conventional paradigms found anomalous.

This scale of investment, measured in millions of dollars equivalent at
the time and involving top-tier scientific talent under controlled
laboratory conditions, reflects a serious commitment to exploring the
informational and energetic capacities of living systems. The effort
stands as one of the most ambitious national attempts to bring frontier
bioenergetic questions into a materialist, measurable scientific
framework.

Overall Framing and Terminology (Background Section)

Soviet researchers deliberately avoided Western occult associations.
They framed the field as the study of informational and energetic
interactions of living organisms with each other and the environment
that do not rely on known sensory channels or conventional
energy-transfer mechanisms.

Key distinctions they used:

- Biocommunications (umbrella term)
- Bioinformation — telepathy, precognition, clairvoyance
- Bioenergetics — psychokinesis, dowsing, bioenergetic therapy, effects
  on matter

Czechoslovak researchers preferred psychotronics, defined as the study
of psychosomatic phenomena that exceed ordinary somatic bases
(autosuggestion, hypnosis, telepathy, psychokinesis, etc.). A
comparative table in the report maps U.S. terms (parapsychology, ESP,
psi) against the Soviet/Czech vocabulary.

The report notes that after a Stalin-era suppression (research largely
halted after 1937 as “mystical”), work resumed in the late 1950s–1960s,
catalyzed by Leonid Vasiliev’s publications. Conferences in the 1960s
showed initial openness; later years saw ideological criticism mixed
with continued technical investigation. Younger researchers shifted
emphasis from pure phenomenology toward instrumentation, quantification,
modeling, and potential engineering applications.

PART I: Extrasensory Perception / Biocommunications

Section I – Background
Reviews historical continuity from pre-revolutionary Russian and early
Soviet work (Bekhterev, Ivanov-Smolensky, Kazhinsky, Vasiliev) through
the post-1950s revival. Emphasizes the move from philosophical debate to
interdisciplinary laboratory science involving physiology, physics, and
cybernetics.

Section II – Telepathy (Energy Transfer) in Animals
Covers classic experiments (Bekhterev–Durov dog suggestion work) and
later studies on biological radiation, UV-mediated energy patterns from
damaged cells (Novosibirsk work), magnetic-field effects on EEG and
hypothalamus (Kholodov), and electromagnetic communication hypotheses
(Presman). Czech researcher Jiri Bradna’s long-running (1948–1968)
myotransfer experiments demonstrated contactless stimulus transfer
between frog neuromuscular preparations mediated by VHF-range energy;
the effect could be blocked, reflected, or guided, and was applied in
physiotherapy. These studies treated inter-organism energy exchange as a
measurable biophysical process rather than a purely mental one.

Section III – Telepathy (Energy Transfer) in Man

- Classical theories and experiments: Electromagnetic, “metaethereal,”
  psychic-energy, and gravitational hypotheses are reviewed.
  Distance-independent transmission and lack of conventional shielding
  effects created theoretical challenges. I. M. Kogan’s
  information-theoretic analyses reported transmission rates on the
  order of 0.005–0.1 bits per second, decreasing with distance.
- Current theories and objectives: Czech models (Wolf’s psychotronic
  model of man with somatic, psychic, and psychotronic spheres;
  Cernousek’s regression/empathy theory; Kahuda’s “mental ions” or
  “mentions” with proposed velocities greater than light and associated
  energy formulas) attempted to place the phenomena inside extended
  physical or informational frameworks. Emphasis shifted toward
  reproducibility, cybernetic modeling, and possible man–machine
  interfaces.

Section IV – Telepathic Behavior Modification
Reviews Vasiliev’s earlier mental-suggestion work (motor acts, images,
sleep–wake induction over distances) and later physiological correlates
(EEG changes, blood-cell count shifts, plethysmographic responses). The
report notes potential applied interest in influencing states at a
distance, while stressing the need for better quantification.

PART II: Psychotronic Generator Research

This section focuses on the work of Czech inventor Robert Pavlita. His
devices—variously shaped metal forms (steel, bronze, etc.)—were said to
collect, store, and re-emit biological or “psychotronic” energy that
could be activated by touch or concentration, even by non-gifted
individuals. Reported effects included influence on plant growth,
precipitation of pollutants, induction of hibernation or death in
insects, and disruption of human EEG or equilibrium under certain
conditions.

The generators were tested under conditions intended to exclude ordinary
electrostatic, magnetic, thermal, and air-current explanations. Czech
researchers viewed them as a practical route to making bioenergetic
effects accessible beyond rare gifted subjects. The report highlights
both the technological ambition and the dual-use implications
(beneficial medical or industrial applications versus potential
antipersonnel effects). Pavlita’s long-term, systematic craftsmanship
and the institutional attention given to the devices illustrate the
seriousness with which the Czech program approached engineering of
biological energy.

PART III: Psychokinesis Research

Soviet work concentrated on a small number of highly gifted subjects
(notably Nina Kulagina and Alla Vinogradova), while Czech work
(including Julius Krmessky’s rotational indicators) emphasized that
effects might be more widely inducible, especially with generators.

I. M. Kogan’s “The Information Theory Aspect of Telepathy” (RAND Corporation Paper P-4145, July 1969)

is a translated analysis of Soviet telepathy experiments that applies
classical information theory to what had previously been treated largely
as qualitative or anecdotal phenomena. Kogan, then chairman of the
Bioinformation Section of the Popov Society in Moscow, examined
controlled laboratory trials involving the transmission of simple
targets (objects, images, or binary-like choices) over both short and
long distances. His central contribution was to calculate actual
information-transfer rates rather than simply reporting “hits” or
subjective impressions.

The paper reports measured transmission rates typically falling between
approximately 0.005 and 0.1 bit per second. Short-distance experiments
tended toward the higher end of that range (around 0.1 bit/sec), while
longer-range trials (hundreds to thousands of kilometers) produced lower
average rates, on the order of 0.005 bit/sec. Kogan treated these
figures as empirical estimates derived from the statistical success
rates and the informational complexity of the targets used. He argued
that the observed rates, though extremely low by ordinary communication
standards, were still consistent with the formal laws of information
transmission and did not require abandoning physical principles
outright.

  In statistics, when you’re looking for forced choice or time-locked
  binary physiological spikes over hundreds of thousands of trials, the
  baseline chance of a random simultaneous spike is drastically lower
  than 50%. A sustained 50% match across a 7 kilometer gap through a
  Faraday cage is a massive statistical anomaly. It proved to the Soviet
  military apparatus that a signal, some kind of high penetrating
  biological radiation or field, was connecting these living systems.

Kogan’s approach was significant because it attempted to place Soviet
telepathy research inside a quantitative, engineering-style framework.
Instead of focusing solely on whether telepathy “existed,” he asked how
much information, if any, was being transferred and under what
conditions the rate changed. This quantitative framing later appeared in
Western intelligence assessments (including the 1975 DIA report on
Soviet and Czechoslovakian parapsychology) as evidence that at least
some Soviet researchers were trying to move the field from phenomenology
toward measurable parameters. The paper remains one of the clearest
contemporary attempts from that era to subject claimed bioinformation
phenomena to the tools of communication theory.

CIA Translation of “Biostimulation through Laser Radiation and Bioplasma” 

Document: CIA-RDP96-00787R000500080001-0, This declassified CIA document
is an English translation of a Soviet monograph by physicist Viktor M.
Inyushin (Kazakh State University, Alma-Ata) and collaborator P. R.
Chekerov. It presents their research on the biological effects of
low-intensity laser radiation, particularly monochromatic polarized red
light (often helium-neon lasers), and links those effects to the concept
of “bioplasma.”

Inyushin and Chekerov argue that living organisms contain a distinct
form of organized energy they call bioplasma—a coherent, plasma-like
state of matter arising from cellular and molecular processes. They
propose that this bioplasma can be stimulated or modulated by weak laser
radiation, producing measurable physiological changes. The monograph
reviews experimental observations of biostimulation in tissues,
nervous-system responses, and potential therapeutic applications,
framing laser light as a tool for influencing biological energy
processes rather than simply a thermal or photochemical agent.

The work sits at the intersection of Soviet laser biophysics and the
broader psychoenergetics / bioenergetics research of the 1960s–1970s. It
was of interest to Western intelligence because it supplied a
materialist theoretical framework (bioplasma) that Soviet researchers
used to discuss phenomena ranging from cellular stimulation to more
speculative energetic effects. The translation preserves the authors’
technical claims, experimental orientation, and emphasis on practical
medical and agricultural applications while reflecting the period’s
attempt to bring unconventional biological-energy concepts into a
quasi-physical model.

Overall, the document is a primary example of the theoretical and
experimental literature that underpinned Soviet interest in bioenergetic
phenomena during the Cold War era.

Experiments in Mental Suggestion by Leonid L. Vasiliev

Leonid Leonidovich Vasiliev’s Experiments in Mental Suggestion remains
one of the most important and carefully documented primary sources from
the Soviet scientific investigation of telepathy and distant mental
influence. Written by a professional physiologist at Leningrad State
University, the book presents decades of laboratory work with unusual
seriousness and restraint. Vasiliev does not traffic in dramatic claims;
instead he offers precise descriptions of experimental design, subject
responses, and the conditions under which effects appeared.

The core of the book examines the ability of one person (the agent) to
influence another (the percipient) at a distance. Vasiliev and his
colleagues tested the mental suggestion of simple motor acts, the
generation of specific visual images or bodily sensations, and the
induction of sleep or waking states. In some series the distance between
participants ranged from a few rooms to many kilometers. The experiments
often included physiological monitoring and careful attempts to control
sensory leakage. What emerges is a picture of intermittent but recurring
effects that the researchers treated as real phenomena requiring further
physiological explanation rather than dismissal or mystical
interpretation.

One of the book’s greatest strengths is its historical and
methodological breadth. Vasiliev situates his own work within the wider
landscape of research into mind-to-mind communication before, during,
and after the Second World War, giving readers a sense of continuity
with earlier Russian investigations as well as contemporary efforts. The
tone throughout is that of a laboratory scientist: measured,
detail-oriented, and more interested in establishing reliable conditions
than in proving a preconceived theory. This scientific posture makes the
volume especially valuable for modern readers who want primary data
rather than second-hand summaries.

The English translation preserves both the technical content and the
sober style of the original. For anyone studying the history of
parapsychology, Soviet biocommunications research, or the broader
question of whether consciousness can act at a distance under controlled
conditions, Vasiliev’s book is essential reading. It stands as a model
of how a serious physiologist approached anomalous mental phenomena with
patience, experimental discipline, and intellectual honesty.

healing and life changes.

“Biophoton Change by Mental Concentration” 

This 2001 study investigates whether focused mental concentration can
alter the ultra-weak light emissions known as biophotons that living
tissue spontaneously radiates. Using a sensitive biophoton imaging
system built around an image intensifier and CCD camera, the researchers
measured light output from the fingertip of seven healthy adult
volunteers while the subjects either concentrated on the idea of light
radiating from that fingertip or performed a control task of silently
counting. The work sits within the broader field of life-information
science and attempts to link subjective mental effort to an objective,
physical marker of biological activity.

The experimental design was straightforward and carefully controlled for
ambient light. Subjects first rested in a darkroom, then completed two
ten-minute measurement periods (mental concentration or control, order
counterbalanced), with brief rests between. Biophoton images were
accumulated over the full ten-minute intervals, allowing total
luminosity to be compared across conditions. Post-session interviews
assessed how successfully each participant felt they had concentrated.
The protocol was repeated on multiple days for most subjects, providing
a modest degree of within-subject replication.

Results showed that mental concentration did produce measurable changes
in fingertip biophoton intensity relative to the control condition.
These changes were not uniform: the seven subjects fell into two clear
groups. In one group biophoton emission increased during concentration;
in the other it decreased. The direction of change was consistent across
the repeated sessions for each individual, suggesting a stable personal
response pattern rather than random fluctuation. Several participants
noted in interviews that the ten-minute concentration period was
demanding, which may have influenced the magnitude of the observed
effects.

The study’s principal contribution is the demonstration that a purely
mental task can modulate an ultra-weak optical signal generated by
living tissue. By classifying subjects into two response types, the
authors highlight individual differences that future work could
explore—differences possibly related to concentration skill,
physiological baseline, or other still-unidentified factors. The use of
a commercial-grade biophoton imaging system also adds technical
credibility, showing that such subtle measurements are feasible under
ordinary laboratory conditions.

While the sample size is small and the precise biophysical mechanism
remains unexplained, the paper offers a clean, replicable demonstration
of mind–body interaction at the level of spontaneous photon emission. It
stands as a useful incremental contribution to research on biophotons
and mental influence, inviting larger studies that could test whether
the two observed response patterns hold in broader populations and
whether training in concentration can systematically alter the direction
or strength of the effect.

“A Preliminary Study of the Effect of External Qigong on Lymphoma Growth in Mice” 

This pilot investigation set out to test whether external qigong emitted
by a trained practitioner could influence the in-vivo growth of
transplantable lymphoma cells in mice. The researchers used a classic
tumor model in which SJL/J mice received intravenous injections of
lymphoma cells known to colonize and expand aggressively in lymphoid
tissue. Animals were assigned to one of three conditions: treatment by a
qigong healer, sham treatment performed by an untrained person who
imitated the healer’s movements, or no treatment at all. Lymph-node
weight relative to body weight served as the primary endpoint of tumor
burden.

In the first experiment the qigong-treated mice showed significantly
smaller lymph nodes than both the sham and untreated control groups,
suggesting reduced tumor growth. A second experiment conducted with the
same design produced a similar directional pattern—smaller nodes in the
qigong group—yet the differences did not reach statistical significance,
largely because of greater variance across all groups. The authors
interpret the combined results as preliminary evidence that the
particular practitioner’s external qigong may have exerted a negative
influence on lymphoma progression under these conditions.

The study’s strengths lie in its controlled design, inclusion of a sham
arm to address expectancy and nonspecific factors, and use of a
quantifiable biological outcome rather than subjective reports. By
working with an established murine lymphoma model, the investigators
placed an unconventional intervention into a conventional experimental
framework, making the data more accessible to biomedical readers. The
work also highlights the practical difficulties of standardizing an
operator-dependent intervention such as external qigong.

Limitations are openly acknowledged. Sample sizes were modest, only one
healer was tested, and the second trial failed to achieve statistical
significance. Mechanisms remain unknown, and the possibility of subtle
differences in handling or environmental factors cannot be entirely
ruled out. The authors therefore frame their findings as suggestive
rather than conclusive and call for replication with additional
practitioners, larger cohorts, and alternative tumor models.

Overall, the paper represents a careful early attempt to bring external
qigong into the domain of controlled animal oncology research. While the
results are preliminary and incomplete, they provide a methodological
template and a rationale for further investigation of whether focused
human intention or biofield practices can modulate malignant growth in
vivo.

There Is Something Here

The high-penetrating emission documented across early qi research in
China to nearly a century of Soviet and Russian research is one of the
most intriguing open questions in modern science. Far from fringe
speculation, it rests on repeated laboratory observations of a radiation
that passes through Faraday cages and dense metal, reflects and
diffracts like light, yet refuses to bend in electrostatic fields, and
consistently influences living systems and material microstructure. What
began as investigations into distant biological signaling has matured
into an instrumental science of generators, sensors, and measurable
effects, inviting every serious researcher to look again at the living
continuum that classical traditions once called Qi.

[]

Decades of state-supported Soviet work produced coherent patterns rather
than isolated anomalies. Turlygin’s optical-style experiments in the
1930s already mapped reflection, diffraction, and penetration delays.
Chizhevsky linked solar rhythms to blood and bacteria. Later teams under
Akimov and Shipov built spin-based devices that altered seed
germination, structured water, shifted material conductivity, and
registered non-local correlations without ordinary electromagnetic
channels. The transition from human operators to hardware detectors
marks the shift from anecdote to experiment; the data set is large,
multi-generational, and still growing in independent laboratories today.

Five strong theoretical frames currently compete to explain what this
emission may be. The first treats it as a torsion or spin-torsion field
arising from rotational degrees of freedom in the physical vacuum
itself, a macroscopic extension of ideas first sketched by Shipov and
engineered by Akimov. The second sees it as an information-bearing
continuum native to the vacuum—an ordered, non-local substrate that can
carry structured signals across ordinary barriers much as the classical
concept of Qi is said to circulate pattern and vitality. The third
frames the phenomenon as a macroscopic quantum effect, possibly related
to Aharonov-Bohm potentials or large-scale entanglement, in which phase
and spin information propagate without energy transfer in the usual
sense.

A fourth theory links the emission to the biophotonic and morphogenetic
fields long studied by Gurvich and others, suggesting that living
systems both generate and respond to a subtle organizing radiation that
couples cellular processes across distance. The fifth, more geometric
proposal holds that we are detecting a rotational or chronal aspect of
spacetime itself—echoing Kozyrev’s asymmetric mechanics—wherein time and
process possess a measurable field character that ordinary
electromagnetism simply does not capture. Each of these frameworks
remains provisional, yet each accounts for substantial portions of the
experimental record without immediate contradiction of established
physics.

What makes the subject compelling is not any single theory but the
convergence of evidence from independent methods: conductivity sensors,
crystallization assays, biological indicators, and passive geometric
generators all point to the same penetrating, information-rich
influence. The research posture required is therefore neither credulous
acceptance nor reflexive dismissal, but disciplined curiosity. New
instruments—precision spin sensors, ultra-stable interferometers,
